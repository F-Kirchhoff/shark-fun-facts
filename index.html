Six things about sharks

Older than trees, and still swimming

Sharks have been in the ocean for about 450 million years. Here is a small collection of facts that make that easier to believe.

One of them lived for 400 years

The Greenland shark is the longest-lived vertebrate we know of, with an estimated lifespan of 250 to 500 years. It swims slowly through near-freezing water and does not have young of its own until it is around 150.

They can feel your heartbeat

Small jelly-filled pores on a shark's snout, called ampullae of Lorenzini, pick up the faint electric fields that every moving muscle gives off. A shark can use them to find a fish hiding under sand, with no need to see it.

Losing a tooth costs them nothing

Behind every tooth sits a row of spares, waiting to rotate forward like a conveyor belt. Some species work through more than 30,000 teeth in a lifetime.

The biggest one eats the smallest food

A whale shark can reach 18 metres, which makes it the largest fish in the ocean. It feeds by filtering plankton and tiny fish out of the water, and it is harmless to swimmers.

They are older than Saturn's rings

Sharks appeared roughly 100 million years before the first trees, and long before Saturn's rings formed. They have survived all five mass extinctions since.

One takes bites out of whales

The cookiecutter shark is about 50 centimetres long, roughly the size of a cat. It latches onto much larger animals and twists free with a neat round plug of flesh, leaving a scar like a cookie cutter.
